## Q1. For each of the four failure modes, what is the correct GDPR characterisation and the Art. 33 / Art. 34 decision tree, per EDPB Guidelines 9/2022?

### Takeaway
GDPR notification is risk-thresholded and fact-dependent, not automatic. A personal data breach exists only where a *breach of security* actually led to destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to personal data (Art. 4(12)); a vulnerability that was never exploited is an Art. 32 weakness, not by itself a notifiable breach. Once a breach exists, Art. 33 requires notification to the supervisory authority within 72 hours of *awareness* "unless the personal data breach is unlikely to result in a risk", Art. 34 requires communication to individuals only where the breach is "likely to result in a *high* risk", and Art. 33(5) requires internal documentation of *every* breach, notifiable or not. The EDPB treats destruction or unrecoverable loss of personal data as always an availability breach, and even a temporary loss (restored from backup) as a breach that must be documented and risk-assessed. The 72-hour deadline and the "unlikely to result in a risk" threshold are the law in force today; the Digital Omnibus changes (96 hours, "high risk" threshold) remain a Commission proposal.

### Cited Findings

**Statutory text (GDPR, Regulation (EU) 2016/679)** — [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) [prior knowledge for article wording; Art. 33(1) and 34(1) wording verified via EDPB quotation below]
- Art. 4(12): "'personal data breach' means a breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data transmitted, stored or otherwise processed."
- Art. 33(1) as quoted by the EDPB: the controller "shall without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it, notify the personal data breach to the supervisory authority competent in accordance with Article 55, unless the personal data breach is unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Where the notification to the supervisory authority is not made within 72 hours, it shall be accompanied by reasons for the delay." — [EDPB Guidelines 9/2022 v2.0, para 28–29](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf) [verified this pass]
- Art. 34(1) as quoted by the EDPB: "When the personal data breach is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller shall communicate the personal data breach to the data subject without undue delay." — [EDPB Guidelines 9/2022, para 82–83](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf) [verified this pass]
- Art. 33(5) as quoted by the EDPB: "The controller shall document any personal data breaches, comprising the facts relating to the personal data breach, its effects and the remedial action taken. That documentation shall enable the supervisory authority to verify compliance with this Article." — [EDPB Guidelines 9/2022, para 121](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf) [verified this pass]
- Other articles relied on below (wording from prior knowledge, official text at the EUR-Lex link): Art. 5(1)(e) storage limitation; Art. 5(1)(f) integrity and confidentiality ("appropriate security … including protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage"); Art. 24(1) controller accountability (implement appropriate technical and organisational measures "to ensure and to be able to demonstrate" compliance); Art. 25(1) data protection by design and Art. 25(2) by default ("by default personal data are not made accessible without the individual's intervention to an indefinite number of natural persons"); Art. 28(1) and 28(3) processor selection and mandatory contract; Art. 32(1)(b) "ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience", 32(1)(c) "ability to restore the availability and access to personal data in a timely manner in the event of a physical or technical incident", 32(1)(d) "regularly testing, assessing and evaluating the effectiveness" of measures.

**EDPB Guidelines 9/2022 — status and key positions** [verified this pass]
- Version history: v1.0 adopted 10 October 2022 as an "updated version of the previous guidelines WP250(rev.01) adopted by the Working Party 29 and endorsed by the EDPB on 25 May 2018"; v2.0 adopted 28 March 2023 after targeted consultation on breaches at controllers not established in the EEA. — [EDPB Guidelines 9/2022, version history](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Three breach types (para 17): "'Confidentiality breach' – where there is an unauthorised or accidental disclosure of, or access to, personal data. 'Integrity breach' – where there is an unauthorised or accidental alteration of personal data. 'Availability breach' – where there is an accidental or unauthorised loss of access to, or destruction of, personal data." Para 18: a breach can concern all three at once. — [EDPB 9/2022, para 17–18](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Availability breaches (para 19): "A breach will always be regarded as an availability breach when there has been a permanent loss of, or destruction of, personal data." Examples: "where data has been deleted either accidentally or by an unauthorised person … In the event that the controller cannot restore access to the data, for example, from a backup, then this is regarded as a permanent loss of availability." — [EDPB 9/2022, para 19](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Temporary loss (para 20–22): "a security incident resulting in personal data being made unavailable for a period of time is also a type of breach"; planned maintenance is not a breach of security; "a breach involving the temporary loss of availability should be documented in accordance with Article 33(5) GDPR … depending on the circumstances of the breach, it may or may not require notification … the controller will need to notify unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to individuals' rights and freedoms. Of course, this will need to be assessed on a case-by-case basis." Worked contrast: hospital data unavailable even temporarily "could present a risk"; a media company unable to send newsletters for several hours "is unlikely to present a risk". — [EDPB 9/2022, para 20–22](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Ransomware with backups (para 23): "Infection by ransomware … could lead to a temporary loss of availability if the data can be restored from backup. However, a network intrusion still occurred, and notification could be required if the incident is qualified as confidentiality breach (i.e. personal data is accessed by the attacker) and this presents a risk". — [EDPB 9/2022, para 23](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Annex B example iv (ransomware, no backups, data cannot be restored): "Yes, report to the supervisory authority, if there are likely consequences to individuals as this is a loss of availability. Yes, report to individuals, depending on the nature of the personal data affected and the possible effect of the lack of availability". Annex B example (brief outage, customers unable to call): "This is not a notifiable breach, but still a recordable incident under Article 33(5)." — [EDPB 9/2022, Annex B](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Awareness (para 31): a controller is "aware" "when that controller has a reasonable degree of certainty that a security incident has occurred that has led to personal data being compromised." Para 34: after first being informed of a potential breach "the controller may undertake a short period of investigation in order to establish whether or not a breach has in fact occurred. During this period of investigation the controller may not be regarded as being 'aware'. However, it is expected that the initial investigation should begin as soon as possible". Para 35: once aware, "a notifiable breach must be notified without undue delay, and where feasible, not later than 72 hours." — [EDPB 9/2022, para 31–35](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Lost-USB example (para 33): even where it cannot be established whether anyone accessed the data, "such a case has to be notified as there is a reasonable degree of certainty that an availability breach has occurred". — [EDPB 9/2022, para 33](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Phased notification: "Information required for the notification can be provided in phases, but in any event controllers should act on any breach in a timely manner." — [EDPB 9/2022, para 7 and section I.B.2](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Processors (para 44): under Art. 33(2) a processor "must notify the controller 'without undue delay'. … the processor does not need to first assess the likelihood of risk arising from a breach before notifying the controller; it is the controller that must make this assessment". Para 47: a processor serving multiple controllers affected by one incident must report to each. Para 48: a processor may notify on the controller's behalf only if authorised contractually. — [EDPB 9/2022, para 44–48](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)
- Documentation (para 121–122): "Regardless of whether or not a breach needs to be notified to the supervisory authority, the controller must keep documentation of all breaches"; this links to Art. 5(2) accountability and Art. 24, and "Controllers are therefore encouraged to establish an internal register of breaches, regardless of whether they are required to notify or not." — [EDPB 9/2022, para 121–122](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)

**Digital Omnibus (GDPR strand) — status as of 8 October 2026** [verified this pass via secondary sources dated Sept 2026]
- Commission proposal COM(2025) 837 final, procedure 2025/0360(COD), published 19 November 2025. Proposed Art. 33 changes: notification only where a breach is "likely to result in a high risk" (current test: unless "unlikely to result in a risk"); deadline 72 → 96 hours; notification through an EU single entry point run by ENISA under NIS2, with direct notification to the authority until it exists; EDPB to propose a common template and a list of high-risk circumstances, adopted by Commission implementing act and reviewed at least every three years. — [Praxikon, 15 Sept 2026](https://www.praxikon.com/en/avg/digital-omnibus/datalekmelding); [White & Case, 5 Dec 2025](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/eu-digital-omnibus-what-changes-lie-ahead-data-act-gdpr-and-ai-act)

**Decision trees per failure mode (synthesis of the above; the characterisation is mine, the rules are the EDPB's)**

*Failure mode 1 — broken authorisation on an internet-reachable data API (anon/cross-tenant read or write; CVE-2025-48757 pattern, EdTech inverted RLS, Moltbook)*
1. Was personal data in the exposed tables? If no → not a GDPR breach (still an Art. 32 defect and possibly a NIS2/DORA/contract issue).
2. Was there a *breach of security leading to* unauthorised access or disclosure (Art. 4(12))? Evidence of actual reads/writes by unauthorised principals (API logs, researcher demonstration, write-back of a "paid" record) = confidentiality and, where writes occurred, integrity breach (EDPB para 17). If logs cannot exclude access, the EDPB's lost-USB reasoning (para 33) and the "reasonable degree of certainty" test (para 31) point toward treating it as a breach once the exposure is confirmed; a short investigation to establish this is allowed before the clock starts (para 34).
3. Art. 33: notify within 72 h of awareness unless "unlikely to result in a risk". Home addresses, debt amounts, payment details, students who may be minors (the incidents in the ledger) are the kind of data the EDPB's risk factors weigh heavily. Reasons for any delay must accompany a late notification.
4. Art. 34: communicate to individuals only if "likely to result in a high risk" (financial data, special categories, minors, identity-theft potential). Separate higher threshold.
5. Always: document under Art. 33(5) even if not notified (para 121–122).
6. Independently of the breach question, a missing or inverted authorisation policy is strong evidence of an Art. 32(1)(b)/(d) and Art. 25(1) shortfall, but these are risk-based duties ("appropriate" measures), not categorical violations; whether a fine or order follows depends on the facts and on the supervisory authority.

*Failure mode 2 — public-by-default publishing (RedAccess sweep)*
1. Is personal data displayed or retrievable without authentication? If only non-personal corporate data → not a GDPR breach.
2. If personal data is reachable by anyone (indexed or not), this is an *accidental disclosure* and therefore a confidentiality breach (para 17). Art. 25(2) is directly engaged because the data was "made accessible without the individual's intervention to an indefinite number of natural persons" by default.
3. Awareness starts when the controller (the employer, if the employee acted in the course of employment) has a reasonable degree of certainty (para 31); discovery by a researcher or journalist is the paradigm "informed by another source" case (para 34).
4. Art. 33 / 34 / 33(5) as above. Search-engine indexing and the duration of exposure are aggravating factors in the risk assessment.
5. Controller/processor facts: see Q1 Inferences — an employee's personal-account signup does not by itself make the builder a processor of the employer.

*Failure mode 3 — platform-side bug (Base44 auth bypass, Lovable Feb–Apr 2026 regression)*
1. Who is the processor? Only where the platform processes Customer Personal Data under an Art. 28 contract (e.g., Lovable's DPA for Customer Personal Data) is it a processor; for data it handles as independent controller (Lovable's "Service Data" and "Log Data", see Q9), the platform is itself the controller for its own breach. Chat histories containing pasted personal data sit at this boundary and need a lawyer's view.
2. Processor's duty: notify the controller "without undue delay" without first assessing risk (Art. 33(2); para 44). The controller's 72 h clock starts at the controller's awareness, not the platform's (para 31, 46–47).
3. Did the bug *lead to* unauthorised access? Wix stated no evidence of exploitation for Base44 (see incident ledger). Where access cannot be shown or excluded, the controller must still reach a reasoned, documented position; the EDPB expects the investigation to "begin as soon as possible" (para 34).
4. Art. 33 / 34 / 33(5) as above.

*Failure mode 4 — agent destruction or availability loss (SaaStr, PocketOS)*
1. Permanent deletion of personal data that cannot be restored = availability breach "always" (para 19) → Art. 33 unless unlikely to result in a risk; Annex B example iv says notify the authority "if there are likely consequences to individuals" and individuals "depending on the nature of the personal data".
2. Deletion restored from backup = temporary loss of availability; still a breach of security (not planned maintenance), must be documented under Art. 33(5), notifiable only if the unavailability itself presents a risk (hospital vs newsletter contrast, para 22).
3. Fabrication of records (SaaStr: 4,000 fictional people) = integrity breach ("unauthorised or accidental alteration", para 17), assessed for risk in its own right.
4. Art. 32(1)(c) (restore availability "in a timely manner") and 32(1)(b) (resilience) are the design duties engaged; co-located backups deleted by the same credential are evidence against "appropriate" measures but, again, risk-based rather than categorical.

### Inferences
- The original report's sentence "Exposure triggers the Article 33 72-hour notification" should become: "Where an exposure amounts to a personal data breach, the controller must assess risk and, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to individuals, notify the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware; individuals must be told only where the risk is high; every breach must be documented regardless." That is the EDPB position in para 22, 28–35, 82–83 and 121.
- "An agent deleting an unrecoverable database … is an availability breach in its own right" is correct per EDPB para 19 and Annex B example iv, with the qualifier that notification still depends on likely consequences to individuals. The sentence should add that a *recoverable* deletion is a documented-but-often-not-notified breach.
- Controller/processor roles: a personal signup to a builder by an employee creates a contract between the individual and the platform, not between the employer and the platform. Whether the platform is a *processor* for the employer depends on whether it processes personal data on the employer's documented instructions under an Art. 28(3) contract; absent that, the platform may well be acting as an independent controller for whatever it receives (Lovable's own DPA says it is independent controller for Service Data). The employer's exposure is then an uncontrolled disclosure to a third party and an Art. 28/32 failure, not "a disclosure to a processor with no Article 28 contract". This is fact-specific and needs a qualified lawyer.
- The Omnibus would, if adopted as proposed, move failure modes 1–4 from "notify unless unlikely to result in a risk" to "notify only if likely to result in a high risk", which in practice would turn many broken-RLS exposures of low-sensitivity data into documentation-only events. None of that applies today.

### Gaps
- I could not open the EDPB Guidelines 01/2021 on examples regarding personal data breach notification (which contain further worked ransomware/backup cases). The 9/2022 Annex B examples cover the same ground.
- I found no supervisory-authority decision as of October 2026 that applies Art. 33/34 to a vibe-coded or AI-generated application. The absence is confirmed by both the original report and this pass; it is a lag, not a safe harbour.
- The Council register entry ST 15698/25 and the EP draft report (22 June 2026) were not opened; the status statement rests on two September 2026 practitioner pages that agree with each other.

## Q2. How should an app registry relate to Art. 30 records and Art. 35 DPIA screening without claiming one ROPA entry per app?

### Takeaway
Art. 30 records are kept per *processing activity* (purpose, categories of data subjects and data, recipients, transfers, retention, security measures), not per system. A vibe-coded app is an asset that *supports* one or more processing activities, and several apps can support one activity. The registry should therefore link each app to the processing-activity record(s) it serves and capture the facts that feed the Art. 30(1)(g) security-measures description and the Art. 35 screening (new technology, scale, sensitivity, vulnerable subjects), rather than being presented as the ROPA itself. DPIAs are triggered by processing "likely to result in a high risk", not by every new citizen app.

### Cited Findings
- Art. 30(1) GDPR: each controller "shall maintain a record of processing activities under its responsibility" containing, among others, the purposes, categories of data subjects and personal data, categories of recipients, third-country transfers, envisaged retention time limits "where possible", and "where possible, a general description of the technical and organisational security measures referred to in Article 32(1)". — [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) [prior knowledge of wording; official text at link]
- Art. 35(1) GDPR: a DPIA is required "where a type of processing in particular using new technologies, and taking into account the nature, scope, context and purposes of the processing, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons"; Art. 35(3) lists three mandatory cases (systematic and extensive automated evaluation with legal or similarly significant effects; large-scale processing of special categories or criminal data; large-scale systematic monitoring of public areas); Art. 35(4) requires each supervisory authority to publish a list of processing operations requiring a DPIA. — [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) [prior knowledge of wording]
- The audit's statement of the law: "the legal unit is a processing activity. Application inventory is indispensable operational governance but does not map one-to-one to Art. 30 entries. DPIAs under Art. 35 are triggered by likely high-risk processing, not by every new citizen app." — [Independent audit, section F](file:///root/.claude/uploads/5bf0f3f3-ae1d-5f0e-8ab5-7bb4ebb3c04d/87eb829b-Vibe_Coding_Research20-20Independent20Accuracy20Audit.md)
- The EDPB's breach guidance ties Art. 33(5) breach documentation to the Art. 24 accountability duty and says the authority "can request to see these records" — the same accountability logic underpins keeping an app inventory that can be produced on request. — [EDPB 9/2022, para 122](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf) [verified this pass]
- WP29 Guidelines on DPIA (WP248 rev.01, endorsed by the EDPB) set out nine criteria (evaluation/scoring; automated decision-making with legal or similar effect; systematic monitoring; sensitive or highly personal data; large scale; matching/combining datasets; vulnerable data subjects; innovative use or new technological solutions; preventing exercise of a right or use of a service) and the rule of thumb that processing meeting two criteria will generally require a DPIA. — [EDPB page listing endorsed WP29 guidelines](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/general-guidance/endorsed-wp29-guidelines_en) [prior knowledge — not re-opened]
- Digital Omnibus proposal: DPIA practice to be harmonised through EU-level lists of processing that does or does not require a DPIA, a common template and methodology, reviewed every three years — proposal only, not in force. — [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/eu-digital-omnibus-what-changes-lie-ahead-data-act-gdpr-and-ai-act) [verified this pass]
- CSA's June 2026 note recommends a "lightweight, non-punitive registration process, with monitoring for unregistered apps" but does **not** mention DPIA or ROPA at all. — [CSA, The Vibe Coding Governance Gap, 2 June 2026](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-vibe-coding-ai-governance-gap-20260602-csa/) [verified this pass]
- The original report's claim "Each app processing personal data needs a Record of Processing Activities (ROPA) entry" rests on a vendor blog (heyData), not on the Regulation or regulator guidance. — [Original report, line 134](file:///root/.claude/uploads/5bf0f3f3-ae1d-5f0e-8ab5-7bb4ebb3c04d/930c3851-Vibe_coding_data_leakage_controls.md)

### Inferences
- Recommended registry-to-GDPR mapping (mine): registry fields *owner, purpose, data categories, data-subject categories, audience, data sources/connectors, hosting region, retention, expiry* → link each app to an existing processing-activity record by purpose; where no record exists the app has surfaced a *new processing activity* and the DPO creates one. The app's authorisation model, publishing setting, backup arrangement and vendor DPA status feed the Art. 30(1)(g) security description and the Art. 28 recipient entry.
- DPIA screening belongs in the registry as a *questionnaire*, not an automatic trigger: new technology (LLM features, agentic actions), sensitive data, minors or other vulnerable subjects, scale, matching of datasets, and systematic monitoring are the WP248 criteria that a citizen-built app most often meets. Two or more → DPIA likely; the national Art. 35(4) list should be checked for the controller's lead authority.
- The registry is nonetheless the strongest *evidence* of Art. 24 accountability available when an authority asks about an app that leaked, which is the original report's underlying (and sound) point.

### Gaps
- No supervisory authority has published guidance that treats an inventory of citizen-built or AI-generated apps as a specific accountability artefact; the position above is derived from the general accountability provisions.
- National Art. 35(4) DPIA lists were not surveyed in this pass.

## Q3. Is a vibe-coded internal CRUD tool an "AI system" under the AI Act? What if it embeds an LLM chat feature? What applies now (Art. 4, Art. 50) and what is deferred?

### Takeaway
No. The AI Act regulates the *deployed system's* behaviour, not how its source code was authored. Under Art. 3(1) and the Commission's February 2025 guidelines, a conventional CRUD application that executes rules "defined solely by natural persons" and does no inference is outside the definition, regardless of whether an AI tool wrote it. The coding assistant itself is an AI system whose provider is the vendor; the enterprise using it is a *deployer*, which is what engages the (softened) Art. 4 AI-literacy duty. If the app embeds an LLM chat or generative feature, that feature is an AI system; the enterprise that integrates it under its own name is likely its provider as well as deployer, and Art. 50(1)/(2) have applied since 2 August 2026 (with a grace period to 2 December 2026 only for the Art. 50(2) marking duty of generative systems already on the market). Annex III high-risk obligations now apply from 2 December 2027 and Annex I from 2 August 2028, following Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (in force 27 July 2026).

- Art. 50(2) — *providers* of AI systems, including GPAI systems, "generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content" must mark outputs in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated, with effectiveness/robustness caveats and an exemption for assistive editing. Trigger: a generative feature in the app. Grace period to 2 December 2026 for systems already on the market before 2 August 2026.
- Art. 50(3) — *deployers* of emotion recognition or biometric categorisation systems must inform exposed persons. Rare in vibe-coded business apps.
- Art. 50(4) — *deployers* generating or manipulating deepfakes must disclose; deployers publishing AI-generated text "to inform the public on matters of public interest" must disclose unless human-reviewed with editorial responsibility.

### Inferences
- Test for the paper: ask three questions of the *deployed* app. (1) Does any component infer outputs (predictions, content, recommendations, decisions) from inputs, rather than executing human-written rules? If no → not an AI system; GDPR/CRA/NIS2 only. (2) If yes, is the enterprise placing that component into service under its own name (e.g., a branded support bot calling an LLM API)? If yes it is likely the *provider* of that AI system (Art. 3(3) "has an AI system … developed and … puts the AI system into service under its own name"), and also the deployer when used internally; Art. 50(1) and, for generative output, 50(2) apply from 2 August 2026. (3) Does the use case fall in Annex III (employment, education, credit, essential services, etc.)? If yes, high-risk obligations apply from 2 December 2027 — the CSA's "drift into high-risk use cases" risk — so the registry should record intended use. Whether an enterprise that merely wraps a third-party GPAI model via API is the "provider" of a generative AI system for Art. 50(2) purposes is contested and depends on the forthcoming code of practice/guidelines; flag for counsel.
- Art. 4 now reads as a duty of effort ("take measures to support the development of AI literacy") with an express no-guarantee clause. For this paper its practical content is: an organisation whose staff use Cursor, Claude Code, Lovable, Replit etc. is a deployer of those AI systems and should be able to show literacy measures proportionate to the staff's role and context; training on BaaS authorisation, secrets and publishing defaults is a natural way to evidence it. Art. 4 is not in the Art. 99 fine catalogue (prior knowledge; verify), so enforcement is via national rules and reputational/accountability routes.
- The CSA note's statement that high-risk enforcement "begins August 2, 2026" was accurate when written (2 June 2026) and is now superseded by Regulation 2026/1744; the writer should not carry that date forward.

- I did not retrieve the verbatim post-Omnibus text of Art. 50(1)–(5) or Art. 99(4); the EUR-Lex HTML blocked extraction. ## Q4. NIS2 (Art. 21) and DORA (Arts. 28–30): do they pull builder platforms and coding SaaS into third-party risk management for in-scope entities, and with what qualifiers?

### Takeaway
Yes, but only for entities already in scope and only through the general supply-chain and secure-development duties — there is no provision naming AI builders or coding assistants. NIS2 Art. 21(2)(d) and (e) require essential and important entities to address supply-chain security (including relationships with direct suppliers and service providers) and "security in network and information systems acquisition, development and maintenance, including vulnerability handling", and Art. 21(3) requires them to consider each supplier's "secure development procedures". DORA requires financial entities to keep a register of *all* ICT contractual arrangements, assess before contracting whether the service supports a critical or important function, and — for such functions — secure unrestricted audit rights, TLPT participation and exit terms (Art. 30(3)), none of which the consumer-grade builder DPAs reviewed in Q9 provide. Both regimes allocate responsibility to the regulated entity, not the vendor.

### Cited Findings

**NIS2 — Directive (EU) 2022/2555** [Art. 21 verified via article mirror this pass; scope/transposition from prior knowledge]
- Art. 21(1): Member States shall ensure essential and important entities take "appropriate and proportionate technical, operational and organisational measures to manage the risks posed to the security of network and information systems which those entities use for their operations or for the provision of their services", proportionate to risk exposure, size, likelihood and severity of incidents. — [NIS2 Art. 21 (mirror)](https://www.nis-2-directive.com/NIS_2_Directive_Article_21.html); [EUR-Lex Directive 2022/2555](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2022/2555/oj/eng)
- Art. 21(2) minimum measures include: (c) "business continuity, such as backup management and disaster recovery, and crisis management"; (d) "supply chain security, including security-related aspects concerning the relationships between each entity and its direct suppliers or service providers"; (e) "security in network and information systems acquisition, development and maintenance, including vulnerability handling and disclosure"; (f) policies to assess effectiveness of measures; (g) "basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training"; (i) "human resources security, access control policies and asset management"; (j) MFA/continuous authentication. — [NIS2 Art. 21 (mirror)](https://www.nis-2-directive.com/NIS_2_Directive_Article_21.html)
- Art. 21(3): when considering (d), entities "shall take into account the vulnerabilities specific to each direct supplier and service provider and the overall quality of products and cybersecurity practices of their suppliers and service providers, including their secure development procedures", and the results of coordinated EU supply-chain risk assessments under Art. 22(1). Art. 21(4): an entity that finds it does not comply must take "without undue delay, all necessary, appropriate and proportionate corrective measures". — [NIS2 Art. 21 (mirror)](https://www.nis-2-directive.com/NIS_2_Directive_Article_21.html)
- Recital 85: entities "should in particular be encouraged to incorporate cybersecurity risk-management measures into contractual arrangements with their direct suppliers". — [EUR-Lex Directive 2022/2555](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022L2555) [verified this pass]
- Incident reporting (Art. 23): early warning within 24 hours, incident notification within 72 hours, final report within one month (recital 102 confirms the 24-hour and one-month steps). — [EUR-Lex Directive 2022/2555, recital 102](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022L2555) [verified this pass]; 72-hour step from prior knowledge.
- Transposition deadline 17 October 2024; scope defined by Annex I (high criticality) and Annex II sectors plus size thresholds; cloud computing service providers and managed service providers are themselves in-scope digital-infrastructure/ICT-service entities. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 (17 October 2024) specifies technical requirements for those digital-infrastructure entities, including secure development life cycle and supply-chain policy requirements. — [EUR-Lex Directive 2022/2555](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2022/2555/oj/eng); [EUR-Lex Implementing Regulation 2024/2690](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2024/2690/oj) [prior knowledge — not re-opened]

**DORA — Regulation (EU) 2022/2554** [Arts. 28 and 30 verified via article mirror; Art. 3 definitions verified at EUR-Lex this pass]
- Art. 3(19): "'ICT third-party service provider' means an undertaking providing ICT services"; Art. 3(21): "'ICT services' means digital and data services provided through ICT systems to one or more internal or external users on an ongoing basis"; Art. 3(22): "'critical or important function' means a function, the disruption of which would materially impair the financial performance of a financial entity" or its continuing compliance/soundness. Recital 63: a "wide range of ICT third-party service providers" including cloud, software, data analytics and data-centre providers. — [EUR-Lex DORA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2554)
- Art. 28(1): financial entities "shall, at all times, remain fully responsible for compliance with" DORA regardless of outsourcing. Art. 28(2): a strategy on ICT third-party risk (non-microenterprises). Art. 28(3): "maintain and update at entity level" a register of information on *all* ICT contractual arrangements, distinguishing those supporting critical or important functions; annual reporting to the competent authority and advance notice of planned critical arrangements. Art. 28(4): before contracting, "assess whether the contractual arrangement covers the use of ICT services supporting a critical or important function", plus supervisory conditions, concentration risk, due diligence on provider suitability and conflicts of interest. Art. 28(5): contract only with providers meeting appropriate information-security standards. Art. 28(7): termination rights for significant breach, material ICT-risk weaknesses, or loss of supervisability. Art. 28(8): documented, tested exit strategies for critical or important ICT services. — [DORA Art. 28 (mirror)](https://www.digital-operational-resilience-act.com/Article_28.html); [EUR-Lex DORA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2554/oj/eng)
- Art. 30(2), all ICT services: (a) clear description of services and subcontracting conditions; (b) regions/countries of service and data processing with advance notice of change; (c) "availability, authenticity, integrity and confidentiality" of data including personal data; (d) access, recovery and return of data in an easily accessible format on insolvency, resolution or termination; (e) service levels; (f) incident assistance; (g) cooperation with authorities; (h) termination rights and notice; (i) participation in security awareness training. — [DORA Art. 30 (mirror)](https://www.digital-operational-resilience-act.com/Article_30.html)
- Art. 30(3), critical or important functions: (a) "precise quantitative and qualitative performance targets"; (b) notice/reporting duties; (c) provider business-continuity plans and security measures; (d) participation in the entity's threat-led penetration testing; (e) "unrestricted" rights of access, inspection and audit for the entity, its appointees and the competent authority, with alternative assurance levels where other clients' rights are affected; (f) mandatory transition period on exit. — [DORA Art. 30 (mirror)](https://www.digital-operational-resilience-act.com/Article_30.html)
- DORA has applied since 17 January 2025 (Art. 64). — [EUR-Lex DORA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2554/oj/eng) [prior knowledge; also stated in the original report]

### Inferences
- NIS2 applicability chain (mine): an in-scope entity's citizen-built app is a "network and information system which [the entity] use[s] for [its] operations" (Art. 21(1)), so its development is governed by 21(2)(e); the builder platform or coding SaaS is a "direct supplier or service provider" under 21(2)(d) and its "secure development procedures" are a 21(3) consideration; the app estate falls under 21(2)(i) asset management; backups under 21(2)(c); training under 21(2)(g). None of this is a *universal* duty — it attaches only to essential/important entities, is proportionate, and is implemented through national transposition that varies.
- DORA applicability chain (mine): a builder platform hosting an app used by a financial entity is an ICT service provider; every such arrangement — including a shadow personal-account signup — belongs in the Art. 28(3) register, and its absence is itself a compliance gap. The decisive qualifier is whether the app supports a *critical or important function*; most citizen prototypes will not, but anything on a payments, onboarding or regulatory-reporting path may. For those, Art. 30(3)(e) requires "unrestricted" audit rights, which Lovable's DPA (no audit clause) and Vercel's DPA (audit rights "satisfied by" SOC 2 reports) do not supply on their standard terms — so the paved-road platform choice for a bank is constrained by contract law before security.
- The audit's warning stands: "A builder may be within third-party due diligence but this cannot be declared as a universal DORA or NIS2 duty for every enterprise and every citizen app."

### Gaps
- No competent authority (national NIS2 authority, EBA/ESMA/EIOPA, or a national financial supervisor) has issued guidance naming AI builders or coding assistants as a supply-chain category; this remains inference from the general provisions.
- Verbatim Art. 23 and Art. 3 NIS2 text and DORA Art. 64 were not extracted from EUR-Lex (page truncation); the article mirrors used are faithful reproductions but secondary.

## Q5. Does the Cyber Resilience Act bring vibe-coded commercial software into scope, and when?

### Takeaway
Only when the software is a *product with digital elements placed on the market* — i.e., made available in the course of a commercial activity as a discrete product (downloadable app, installable software, embedded software, or "software plus cloud" where the remote component is essential). Pure SaaS/web applications used over the internet without local installation are generally outside the CRA and fall instead under NIS2/DORA if the provider or customer is in scope. Internal tools never placed on the market are out of scope. For in-scope products, Art. 14 reporting duties (actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents) apply from 11 September 2026 — already live — and the full essential-requirements, vulnerability-handling, documentation and conformity-assessment regime applies from 11 December 2027.

### Cited Findings
- Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 (CRA) entered into force 10 December 2024 and applies in full from 11 December 2027; vulnerability-handling/reporting obligations take effect 11 September 2026. — [DLA Piper, 16 Feb 2026](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/cyber-resilience-act-the-fine-line-between-saas-and-digital-products); [Kirkland, Sept 2026](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2026/09/the-eu-cyber-resilience-act) [both verified this pass]; official text [EUR-Lex CRA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/2847/oj/eng) [prior knowledge — not re-opened]
- Scope: "products with digital elements," covering hardware and software with a direct or indirect data connection; "Pure SaaS is generally excluded. The exception is 'remote data processing' that is essential to a covered product's core functionality, which requires a case-by-case assessment"; open-source software "developed outside commercial activity" is excluded. — [Kirkland](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2026/09/the-eu-cyber-resilience-act)
- DLA Piper's test: "whether the software is made available as a product placed on the market, not whether it is software." Commission FAQs indicate "software downloadable and installable on a device, such as an app store app or a downloadable program, will be caught." Pure SaaS "meaning cloud-hosted applications used over the internet without local installation and not marketed as a distinct product, is generally outside the CRA"; such services "may instead fall under adjacent regimes such as NIS2 and DORA"; providers "should not assume a free pass". The remote-data-processing concept "is expected to form part of separate guidance from the Commission." — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/cyber-resilience-act-the-fine-line-between-saas-and-digital-products)
- Reporting from 11 September 2026 (Art. 14, to ENISA's Single Reporting Platform): actively exploited vulnerabilities — early warning within 24 hours of awareness, vulnerability notification within 72 hours, final report within 14 days after a corrective measure is available; severe incidents — early warning 24 hours, incident notification 72 hours, final report within one month. Applies to products already on the market. — [Kirkland](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2026/09/the-eu-cyber-resilience-act); [Crowell countdown alert](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/eu-cyber-resilience-act-countdown-11-september-2026-incidentvulnerability-reporting-deadline-is-less-than-100-days-away)
- From 11 December 2027: essential cybersecurity requirements, vulnerability handling, technical documentation, conformity assessment and Art. 13 upstream reporting. — [Kirkland](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2026/09/the-eu-cyber-resilience-act)
- Penalties (Art. 64): up to EUR 15 million or 2.5% of worldwide turnover for essential-requirement and core manufacturer-obligation breaches (including reporting); EUR 10 million / 2% for other obligations; EUR 5 million / 1% for incorrect information. — [Kirkland](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2026/09/the-eu-cyber-resilience-act)
- Commission CRA FAQs were released end-2025 and updated in 2026 (July 2026 per Kirkland). — [Kirkland](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2026/09/the-eu-cyber-resilience-act); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/cyber-resilience-act-the-fine-line-between-saas-and-digital-products)
- Annex I essential requirements (prior knowledge of the 2024 text, not re-opened): products to be made available "without known exploitable vulnerabilities", with "a secure by default configuration", protection from unauthorised access via authentication and access control, confidentiality through encryption, integrity, data minimisation, availability/resilience, logging, and secure update mechanisms; Part II requires vulnerability handling including an SBOM, coordinated disclosure policy, and security updates for the support period (minimum 5 years unless shorter expected use). — [EUR-Lex CRA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/2847/oj/eng)

### Inferences
- For the four failure modes: a vibe-coded *SaaS* product with a Supabase backend is normally outside the CRA, so the CRA is not the lever for broken-RLS exposures in hosted apps; a vibe-coded *mobile or desktop app* distributed commercially (including free, if in the course of commercial activity) is in scope, and its back end may be a "remote data processing solution" if the app cannot function without it. The manufacturer (the enterprise shipping it) then owes Art. 14 reporting *today* and Annex I compliance from December 2027.
- The CRA's Annex I "secure by default configuration" and "no known exploitable vulnerabilities" requirements are the closest statutory expression of the paper's thesis (defaults over prompts), but only for in-scope products; cite them as a benchmark, not as an obligation on hosted web apps.
- Even out-of-scope SaaS vendors (Lovable, Replit, Vercel) are likely to face CRA-like due-diligence questions from in-scope customers, as DLA Piper predicts.

### Gaps
- The Commission's separate guidance on "remote data processing solutions" had not been located as published by October 2026 in this pass; status unknown.
- Whether the Commission FAQ addresses AI-generated or no-code-built software specifically was not checked.

## Q7. US: California CCPA regulations, SEC, NYDFS, HIPAA, FTC, state AI laws

### Takeaway
The US pressure is procedural rather than substantive: California's 2026 CCPA regulations require documented risk assessments (from 1 January 2026, first attestations by 1 April 2028) and independent cybersecurity audits certified on a revenue-staggered schedule (April 2028/2029/2030), which an unregistered vibe-coded app would fail by omission; the SEC and NYDFS regimes make material incidents disclosable and require asset inventories; HIPAA makes a builder that signs no BAA unusable for PHI. The much-discussed Colorado AI Act was repealed and replaced in May 2026 by a narrower transparency statute effective 1 January 2027.

## Q9. Contracts: what to look for in builder DPAs, using Lovable (DPA signed 17 Nov 2025), Vercel, Replit and Cursor as examples

### Takeaway
The three documents actually read in this pass show the same pattern: the vendor is processor only for a defined class of "Customer Personal Data" and independent controller for service/usage/log data; training prohibitions attach to the processor class but not necessarily to the controller class; audit rights are replaced by SOC 2/ISO reports; deletion is "commercially reasonable" or "may persist in backups"; breach notice is "without undue delay" with no hour commitment; sub-processor objection windows are short and the sole remedy is termination. For a financial entity these standard terms fall short of DORA Art. 30(3)(e) audit requirements; for any controller they require a documented Art. 28(3) gap analysis.

### Inferences — procurement checklist (mine)
1. **Controller/processor carve-outs**: identify exactly which data classes the vendor treats as its own controller data (Lovable: Service Data and Log Data; Vercel: Service-Generated and Contact Data). Prompts, chat histories and project source often fall in or near that class; if employees paste personal data into prompts, ask the vendor in writing whether prompt content is Customer Personal Data.
2. **Training**: require the no-training clause to cover all classes the vendor can see, not only "Customer Personal Data"; Lovable's 9.1(c)/10.3 tension is the kind of ambiguity to resolve before signature.
3. **Audit rights**: Art. 28(3)(h) requires the processor to "allow for and contribute to audits, including inspections"; report-only clauses (Lovable, Vercel) may be acceptable for low-risk processing but not for DORA critical/important functions (Art. 30(3)(e) "unrestricted" access) — financial entities need negotiated terms or a different platform.
4. **Backup persistence and deletion**: demand a stated maximum backup retention and a deletion certificate; "may persist in backups for a limited time" and "commercially reasonable timeframe" are open-ended.
5. **Sub-processors**: 5–20 day objection windows with termination as sole remedy mean the controller must monitor the list proactively; map sub-processors to the Art. 30 record.
6. **Transfers**: confirm which SCC module applies (Module Two vs Three depends on whether the customer is controller or processor) and whether any DPF certification is actually relied on.
7. **Breach notification**: "without undue delay after confirming" leaves the controller's 72-hour clock exposed; negotiate a fixed outer bound (e.g., 24–48 hours from the processor's awareness) and a duty to notify on *suspicion*, consistent with EDPB para 44 (processor need not assess risk first).
8. **Regulated data**: no BAA = no PHI; check equivalent exclusions for payment data (PCI DSS responsibility matrix) and for special-category data.

## Q10. Any enforcement, guidance or consultation specifically about AI-generated code or citizen development published by regulators in 2025–2026?

### Takeaway
As of 8–9 October 2026 I found no supervisory-authority enforcement decision, and no EU/UK data-protection-authority guidance, that addresses AI-generated code, vibe coding or low-code/no-code citizen development by name. The nearest regulator statements are the UK NCSC's March 2026 vibe-coding intervention (a cyber agency, not a DPA), the Dutch Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens' August 2024 warning that employees entering personal data into AI chatbots causes data breaches, and the Turkish KVKK's 2025 announcement on generative-AI use in workplaces (non-EU). The CSA explicitly asks regulators to recognise citizen developers as a deployment pathway in AI Act guidance, which confirms the gap.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- The regulatory signal to date comes from cyber agencies (NCSC) and standards bodies (NIST agent initiative, CSA, OWASP), not from DPAs. DPAs have addressed the *input* side (employees pasting personal data into chatbots) under existing Art. 32/breach rules; none has addressed the *output* side (apps built by AI and published by non-developers). The paper can say this precisely rather than "no regulation targets vibe coding by name".
- When a DPA case does arrive, the EDPB's existing framework (Q1) already supplies the analysis; the likely contested points will be awareness timing, whether exposure without proven access is a breach, and whether the employer or the platform is controller for prompt/chat content.
