Data leaks from vibe-coded applications and AI coding agents almost never come from clever-but-wrong generated code; they come from four structural conditions that the generated code cannot see and the builder does not know exist. In 20 incidents and seven large-scale scans verified against primary sources for this paper, the demonstrated capability was row-level read or write through an internet-reachable data API with missing or inverted authorisation, unauthenticated publishing by default, platform-side authorisation bugs, or an agent holding an over-scoped credential that reached production and its backups together. The original research report this paper reviews had the thesis right and roughly thirty specifics wrong or over-compressed; the independent audit that followed caught most of the headline errors but missed at least six, including that Supabase's 30 October 2026 grant change leaves RPC EXECUTE defaults untouched, that the certificate-transparency blind spot it listed as an open question is now empirically verified for all five builder platforms, and that an in-the-wild malicious slopsquat package existed before the audit was written. The corrected practitioner position is that controls bind only at four boundaries the code does not own: builder identity and workspace, published-app ingress, the data and connector API, and the agent execution plane. At each boundary the paper gives the exact semantics, the dated vendor defaults, the behavioural test that proves the control works, and the documented bypass. Generation-time controls such as prompts, rules files and AI review measurably shift the correctness-versus-security trade-off but never cap damage; on the one exploit-confirmed benchmark of real deployed vibe-coded apps, 182 of 200 (91.0%) carried at least one confirmed vulnerability and Broken Access Control appeared in 64.0% of them (Deng, Fan and Meng, arXiv 2606.23130v4). The legal layer is technology-neutral and already reaches every failure mode, but GDPR notification is risk-thresholded rather than automatic, a vibe-coded CRUD tool is not an "AI system" under the AI Act, and NIS2 and DORA duties attach only to in-scope entities and, for DORA's strongest clauses, only to critical or important functions. Everything quantitative or evaluative below carries an evidence-confidence tag; the tiered model, the rollout plan and the KPIs marked as proposals are the authors' design, not measured practice.

1. Review of the original report: what holds, what needed correction

1.1 What holds

The original report's architecture survives intact. Its four failure modes (client-reachable data API with broken authorisation, public-by-default publishing, platform-side bugs, agent destruction with co-located backups) are confirmed by the full incident ledger in section 3, and no incident found in the May–October 2026 extension required a fifth category INF. Its central claim that controls must be enforced by a layer the generated code does not own is supported by every post-incident vendor remediation, all of which changed defaults or blast radius rather than code correctness: Replit's dev/prod database separation (Fortune) PO, Railway's grace period on the API volumeDelete mutation (Railway Central Station) CP, Supabase's explicit-grants default (Supabase changelog 45329) CP, and Lovable's private-by-default projects (Lovable) CP.

Statements that fresh primary-source research confirms verbatim or near-verbatim CP: the Base44 auth/register and auth/verify-otp mechanism and sub-24-hour fix (Wiz); Moltbook's four remediation rounds in roughly three hours (Wiz); the Supabase 30 October 2026 date and the grants-versus-RLS distinction; that Vercel Authentication admits Vercel accounts, not the workforce IdP (Vercel Authentication); that Cloudflare Access and Google IAP fail open unless the origin validates the proxy's token; every Claude Code sandbox seam it listed, now stated in Anthropic's own documentation (Claude Code sandboxing); the Lovable DPA's independent-controller carve-out and no-audit-right position (Lovable DPA); the AI Omnibus dates; the Tea attribution caution (Simon Willison); and the absence of any DPA enforcement decision on a vibe-coded app as of October 2026 INF.

2.3 Where the audit was incomplete or wrong

  1. A3 omitted RPC. The audit listed "security-definer functions, public views and unsafe RPC endpoints" as needing independent inspection but did not state that the 30 October change leaves function EXECUTE defaults untouched, which is the specific reason an agent-created RPC can remain anonymously callable in a project that believes itself "secure by default" CP/INF. It also did not note that the RLS event trigger is opt-in per project, nor that the default-privilege revokes are issued for role postgres and would not cover tables created by another owner role INF.
  2. Wildcard certificates. Listed as a discussion topic (G, "Discovery"); now empirically verified for all five platforms, with the consequence that EASM attribution by certificate name or netblock cannot work for platform subdomains (section 4.6) CP.
  3. Slopsquatting. The unused-imports case (Aikido, 20 Feb 2026) predates the audit; the audit's C7 should have said "one confirmed malicious package, intent probable, impact unquantified" VR.
  4. NCSC attribution. Not caught CP.
  5. Colorado. The audit's US coverage was limited to CCPA; the Colorado AI Act's repeal and replacement (14 May 2026) was five months old at audit date CP.
  6. CSA's superseded date. The audit cited the CSA note without flagging that its 2 August 2026 high-risk date had been overtaken by Regulation 2026/1744 on 27 July 2026 CP.
  7. Smaller misses. The CVE-2025-59536 conflation, the Railway "48-hour" paraphrase, the Netskope 54% mis-attachment, the Retool two-survey conflation and the Gartner-forecast-via-reprint provenance were not raised CP/VR.

None of these reverses an audit conclusion; they are gaps in a review that was explicit about not being line-by-line. The audit's instruction to obtain qualified legal review before publication stands and is repeated in section 11.

3. Four failure modes and the verified incident ledger

3.1 The four failure modes, restated precisely

Failure mode 1 — broken authorisation on an internet-reachable data API. The browser holds an intentionally public key and talks to PostgREST, Firebase or an equivalent. Exposure is decided by grants (may this role touch the table at all), RLS (which rows), column privileges, view and function ownership, and the logic of each policy. The key is not the defect; in every incident of this class the primary source says so CP. The characteristic defects are: no RLS; RLS with a permissive USING (true) policy; TO authenticated with no tenant predicate (authentication without authorisation); inverted logic (the EdTech RPC that "blocks the people it should allow and allows the people it should block" (The Register) PO); and SECURITY DEFINER functions or owner-privileged views that bypass RLS silently (Supabase RLS docs) CP.

Failure mode 2 — public-by-default publishing. Not a code defect. The platform publishes to the open internet unless the builder changes a setting; the app is indexed; nobody in the organisation knows it exists. The RedAccess sweep is the canonical case and the vendors' reply ("a public app is not a breach") is technically correct and is the point (Axios) PO.

Failure mode 3 — platform-side authorisation bugs. Base44's unauthenticated registration endpoints and Lovable's February–April 2026 ownership-check regression share one root cause, authentication without object-level authorisation, and no app-level control could have helped CP/INF. Chat histories, prompts and source trees are data stores held by the platform.

Failure mode 4 — agent credential reach. An agent holding a credential that can mutate production, delete a volume or run terraform destroy does so, usually after a context mismatch, sometimes against explicit instructions. The constant across Replit, Cursor/Claude, Claude Code, Kiro and Antigravity incidents is credential reach, not model vendor INF. Where backups live in the same identity domain as the credential, they go too.

4.3.1 Identity-aware proxies and origin validation

4.4 Boundary B3: the data and connector API

4.4.1 Supabase semantics: schema exposure, grants, RLS, columns, views, RPC

Four independent PostgreSQL mechanisms gate a Supabase Data API request, in this order. First, schema exposure: with the Data API disabled "none of the auto-generated REST endpoints respond, regardless of grants or RLS" (Supabase hardening guide) CP. Second, table and column grants: "Grants decide whether a role can run an operation on the table at all"; "A missing grant raises a 42501 error before any policy runs"; and "Adding policies doesn't take those grants back" (Supabase RLS docs) CP. PostgREST's model is that "All authorization happens in the database"; requests with no JWT run as the anonymous role, and a JWT role claim causes SET LOCAL ROLE for the request (PostgREST auth) CP. Third, RLS: when enabled, "If no policy exists for the table, a default-deny policy is used"; permissive policies combine with OR, restrictive with AND; superusers, BYPASSRLS roles and table owners bypass it; referential-integrity checks "always bypass row security" and can be a covert channel (PostgreSQL ddl-rowsecurity) CP. An UPDATE policy needs a corresponding SELECT policy, and without WITH CHECK "the using expression decides both which rows are visible and which new rows are allowed", so a user can reassign a row's user_id to another user (Supabase RLS docs; Supabase SKILL.md) CP. Authorisation claims must come from app_metadata (raw_app_meta_data), never user_metadata, which "can be modified by authenticated end users" CP. Fourth, column privileges, which work "independently from RLS" but break select * for restricted roles and are labelled "an advanced feature" (Supabase column-level security) CP.

Two side paths bypass all of the above unless configured. Views "bypass RLS by default because they are usually created with the postgres user"; on Postgres 15+ security_invoker = true makes a view obey the invoking user's policies (Supabase RLS docs; PostgreSQL CREATE VIEW) CP. Functions: "By default, any role can run a database function", including anon; a security definer function owned by postgres has bypassrls and "can return rows the caller isn't allowed to read"; without a pinned search_path a caller can hijack an unqualified name (Supabase database functions) CP. The agent skill's rule is blunt: "Never add SECURITY DEFINER to resolve a permission error; it silently removes access control" CP. Security Advisor lints exist for each (0010 security definer view, 0028/0029 security definer functions executable by anon/authenticated, 0011 mutable search path, 0024 permissive policy, 0025 public bucket allows listing) (Supabase database advisors) CP, but lints 0008 and 0024 are presence and pattern checks; they cannot tell whether a predicate matches the tenancy model INF. The GraphQL path (pg_graphql) follows the same grants and RLS; introspection is off by default from 1.6.0; pg_graphql stopped being enabled by default on new projects on 18 May 2026 (Supabase GraphQL; changelog 45329) CP.

4.4.2 What the 30 October 2026 change does and does not do

Changelog 45329 (published 28 April 2026) is precise CP. New tables in public no longer receive the default select, insert, update, delete grants to anon, authenticated and service_role; without an explicit GRANT, PostgREST returns 42501 with the hint "GRANT SELECT ON public.your_table TO anon;". Dates: new default for all new projects 30 May 2026 ("gradual rollout over a few weeks"); applied to all existing projects 30 October 2026. Verbatim limits: "Existing tables are not affected in your project, they keep their current grants and stay reachable"; "RLS behavior remains unchanged"; direct connections "will not affect you"; storage, auth, realtime, custom schemas and self-hosted deployments are out of scope (Supabase changelog 45329).

Three consequences the audit did not draw INF. The changelog's early-adoption SQL revokes default privileges on tables and sequences only; the hardening guide separately revokes EXECUTE on functions from anon, authenticated, service_role and public (Supabase hardening guide) CP. A new RPC function in public therefore stays callable by anon after 30 October unless the project also applies the function revokes; RPC exposure is a separate audit item. The RLS auto-enable event trigger (ensure_rls on ddl_command_end) is a per-database object only postgres can create, covers only public, enables RLS but adds no grants or policies, and "Existing tables still need RLS enabled manually" (Supabase event triggers) CP; its presence on a builder-provisioned project must be verified with select evtname from pg_event_trigger, not assumed. And the default-privilege statements are issued for role postgres; PostgreSQL default privileges are per creating role, so tables created by a different owner role would not be covered (PostgreSQL semantics; not discussed by Supabase). Whether the 30 October rollout for existing projects is instantaneous or gradual like the May rollout is not stated; test after the date INF.

"Secure by default" after 30 October therefore means only that a new table the agent forgets to grant is unreachable via REST and GraphQL. A table the agent does grant to anon with no RLS, or with a USING (true) policy, is as exposed as before. Grants are necessary, not sufficient; RLS is necessary, not sufficient; both plus correct policy logic are required INF. The practical privileged paths to audit on any project are: anything using a secret or service_role key; security definer functions and views in exposed schemas; custom roles with bypassrls; direct Postgres connection strings held by apps or agents INF.

Key model: publishable keys (sb_publishable_) are designed to be public and map to anon or authenticated; secret keys (sb_secret_) map to service_role, bypass RLS and are rejected with HTTP 401 when sent from a browser; legacy JWT anon and service_role keys are "deprecated by the end of 2026" with removal "Late 2026, TBC", and projects restored since 1 November 2025 receive no legacy keys (Supabase API keys; changelog 29260) CP. The OpenAPI schema at /rest/v1/ is no longer served to anon callers since 8 April 2026 for all projects (changelog 42949) CP, which removes the enumeration step Wiz used at Moltbook but not the data path.

4.4.3 Trust-path analysis: direct-to-BaaS with verified RLS versus a least-privilege BFF

The question is not "browser or server" but "where is the authorisation decision bound to the caller's identity and tenant on every request". Four paths INF, each grounded in vendor documentation CP:

Path A — browser to PostgREST with a publishable key, verified grants and RLS. The enforcement point is Postgres, which sees the real sub and claims on every request and cannot be bypassed by the client. Weaknesses: policy-logic correctness (needs the tests in 4.4.4); privileged side paths (security-definer RPC, non-invoker views, public buckets); no natural place for rate limiting, business validation or audit beyond db-pre-request hooks, which the hardening guide describes for "rate limiting, extra API key checks, or blocking direct access to certain objects" (Supabase hardening guide). An internet-reachable API is inherent.

Path B1 — BFF forwarding the user's JWT. Supabase Edge Functions default to verify_jwt = true; auth: 'user' yields a client "scoped to the caller's Row Level Security (RLS) policies" (Supabase Edge Functions auth). With a secret key, "A secret key bypasses RLS only when the request carries no user access token"; with a user token present the request "runs under the RLS policies of that signed-in user" (Supabase RLS docs). This is the least-privilege BFF: RLS remains the enforcement point and the server tier adds rate limiting, audit, validation and composition. Compromise of the BFF yields only the current caller's rights unless the secret key is also exfiltrated.

Path B2 — BFF with a secret key and no user token. RLS is removed from the path; tenant binding depends on every query carrying the right filter; one missing where org_id = ... is a cross-tenant leak, and a BOLA on any endpoint exposes the whole database. Supabase's own guidance treats this as the exception: "ctx.supabaseAdmin bypasses Row Level Security, so filter by the caller's ID"; "Use ctx.supabaseAdmin only for work that has to cross those policies" (Supabase Edge Functions auth). This is the confused deputy the audit described and is strictly weaker than Path A unless the BFF's authorisation is itself verified by the same cross-tenant tests.

Path B3 — BFF with a dedicated Postgres role over a direct connection. The 30 October change does not apply; grants and RLS must be designed explicitly; the role must not have BYPASSRLS and must not own the tables (owners bypass RLS unless FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY); per-request identity can be propagated with SET LOCAL ROLE and set_config('request.jwt.claims', ...) in a transaction, mirroring PostgREST (PostgreSQL ddl-rowsecurity).

Authorisation engines (Cedar, OPA, OpenFGA, Oso) are decision points, not enforcement points: Cedar defaults to Deny (Cedar), OPA "decouples policy decision-making from policy enforcement" (OPA), Oso returns "a boolean, a list, or logic to run against your database" (Oso) CP. In Path A they are irrelevant; in Path B their decision must be reflected in the query predicate, otherwise the engine approves while the secret-key query over-fetches INF. The OWASP Authorization Cheat Sheet supplies the BFF's acceptance criteria: deny by default, validate on every request, never rely on client-side checks, log, test (OWASP) CP. Governed low-code platforms implement Path B1/B3 by construction: Superblocks apps "never connect directly to business systems" (Superblocks); Retool permissions are per resource and group (Retool); ServiceNow Build Agent apps inherit ACLs (ServiceNow) CP. No vendor publishes breach-outcome comparisons between direct-RLS and BFF architectures; this analysis is reasoning from documented semantics INF.

4.4.4 The behavioural authorisation test matrix

A presence check ("RLS enabled", "policy exists") passed the EdTech app and would pass a USING (true) policy. The test that matters is behavioural: three principals (anonymous publishable-key caller, authenticated tenant-A user, authenticated tenant-B user), four surfaces (REST table, GraphQL if enabled, each RPC function, Storage object), five operations (select, insert, update, upsert, delete), asserting on row counts and SQLSTATE, not HTTP status PROP assembled from documented patterns CP. The assertion discipline is the point: an RLS-filtered SELECT succeeds with zero rows and an UPDATE or DELETE of invisible rows succeeds affecting zero rows, so a test that checks only for 200/204 passes on a broken policy; a 42501 signals a missing grant or a WITH CHECK violation and is the correct expectation for anonymous access to tenant data after 30 October INF.

Documented tooling as of October 2026: pgTAP via supabase test db, with set local role and set local "request.jwt.claims" for impersonation (Supabase testing overview) CP; the basejump supabase_test_helpers extension 0.0.6 (tests.create_supabase_user, tests.authenticate_as, tests.clear_authentication, tests.rls_enabled) (Supabase pgTAP extended) CP; Supabase's RLS Tester feature preview (24 April 2026), which is SELECT-only and therefore cannot detect missing WITH CHECK or UPDATE-without-SELECT defects (changelog 45233) CP; SupaShield, a community CLI that generates anonymous and authenticated CRUD tests in rolled-back transactions (GitHub) RR; and Lovable's Deep scan with Aikido "dynamic AI penetration testing" (Lovable security) VR. Application-level HTTP tests cannot use transactions for isolation and must sign in real users (Supabase testing overview) CP.

4.4.6 Bundle secrets: what each control actually scans

Framework prefixes inline values into the browser bundle by design: Next.js "inline[s]" NEXT_PUBLIC_ values at build time; Vite says VITE_* variables "should not contain sensitive information such as API keys" (Next.js; Vite) CP. 4.5 Boundary B4: the agent execution plane

4.5.1 What is enforced outside the model, and where the seams are

Across Claude Code, Copilot coding agent, Cursor, Codex, Windsurf/Devin Desktop and Replit, the only controls enforced by something other than the model are an OS-level sandbox around shell commands, a network proxy allowlist in front of those commands, cloud-side isolation (ephemeral runners, branch and token restrictions), and the IAM or OAuth scope of the credentials the agent holds. Every vendor documents seams in its own words CP.

Claude Code. The sandbox is "off by default"; when on, writes are limited to the working directory and a temp directory, but reads cover "Most of the machine, including credential files such as ~/.ssh and ~/.aws/credentials", environment variables are "Inherited from Claude Code, including any secrets in its environment", and the network allowlist "start[s] empty" (Claude Code sandboxing) CP. "The sandbox covers shell commands only. Claude's file tools, MCP servers, and hooks run outside it"; "A denyRead entry doesn't stop the Read tool"; hooks, local MCP servers, LSP servers and apiKeyHelper "run with your full access" CP. Fail-open: if the sandbox cannot start "Claude Code runs commands without sandboxing" unless failIfUnavailable is true CP. Unsandboxed retry: the model "may retry the command with the dangerouslyDisableSandbox parameter", and "if an allow rule such as Bash(curl *) matches the command, it also approves the retry, so the command runs outside the sandbox with no prompt"; disable with allowUnsandboxedCommands: false CP. There is "no built-in credential deny list" CP. Bash deny rules match command text and are "not a security boundary around the program": Bash(curl *) does not stop /usr/bin/curl or sh -c 'curl ...' (Claude Code permissions) CP. Native Windows runs unsandboxed CP. Anthropic's own remedy for the seams is to run the whole process "in a container, virtual machine, or the sandbox runtime" CP. Practitioner traces confirmed the Read-tool gap, default reads of /etc/passwd and SSH keys, the retry prompt text "(don't ask, just do it)", and exfiltration through gists once github.com is allowlisted (Claude Code Camp) RR. Three pre-trust execution paths were fixed between August and December 2025: hooks in .claude/settings.json (GHSA-ph6w-f82w-28w6, no CVE), MCP servers from .mcp.json before approval (CVE-2025-59536, fixed 1.0.111) and ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL exfiltration (CVE-2026-21852, fixed 2.0.65) (Check Point Research; NVD) CP. Opening an untrusted repository remains an attack surface; Check Point's advice is to "Inspect configuration directories such as .claude/ and .vscode/ before opening projects" CP.

GitHub Copilot coding agent. The firewall is on by default with a recommended allowlist covering OS package repositories, container registries and language package registries; "The firewall applies only to processes the agent starts through its Bash tool. It does not directly apply to MCP server processes or processes started in configured setup steps"; it "should not be considered a comprehensive security solution" (GitHub firewall) CP. Platform-enforced guardrails: no push to the default branch, one copilot/ branch per task, Actions on agent PRs need write-user approval, hidden-character filtering, secrets only from the copilot environment, 59-minute sessions (GitHub responsible use; About coding agent) CP. Once an MCP server is configured "Copilot can use its tools autonomously without asking for approval", so GitHub recommends read-only tool allowlists rather than "*" (GitHub MCP) CP.

Cursor, Codex, Windsurf/Devin, Replit. Cursor's sandbox (2.0, macOS Seatbelt) defaults to readBoundary: "system" (reads unrestricted) and networkPolicy.default: "deny"; team-admin policies "can't be weakened by user or repo files"; Auto-review "is not a security boundary" and a failed sandboxed command can be rerun outside the sandbox after classifier review (Cursor sandbox.json; Cursor Run modes) CP. Codex defaults to workspace-write, offers danger-full-access, and its domain-enforcing proxy "Defaults to false" with "App and connector traffic ... not controlled" by it (Codex config) CP. Windsurf/Devin Desktop documents prefix-matched allow/deny lists up to a Turbo mode and no OS sandbox or network restriction (Devin Desktop) CP. Replit's "Agent is not able to modify the production database" (Replit dev/prod) CP is the only vendor change in the record that removes the destructive capability rather than mitigating it INF.

Every documented allowlist (Copilot's recommended list, Claude Code's github.com, Cursor defaults) includes hosts that accept uploads; a network allowlist bounds where exfiltration can go, not whether it can happen INF.

4.5.2 MCP: the host-side channel every sandbox leaves open

The lethal trifecta is private data, untrusted content and an exfiltration channel; "LLMs are unable to reliably distinguish the importance of instructions based on where they came from" (Simon Willison) RR. The General Analysis demonstration (8 Jul 2025) had Cursor plus the Supabase MCP under service_role read a support ticket submitted via anon, then copy integration_tokens back into the ticket; read-only mode "blocks the write-back step but still permits reads" (General Analysis) RR. Supabase's MCP page (8 Oct 2026) says to connect to production "only when the task requires production evidence", with read_only=true, project_ref scoping and restricted feature groups; manual approval of each tool call is "a guardrail, not a guarantee of human review"; result-wrapping against injection "is not foolproof" (Supabase MCP) CP. Tool poisoning (hidden instructions in tool descriptions, "rug pulls", cross-server shadowing) exfiltrated ~/.cursor/mcp.json and SSH keys in Invariant's demonstration (Invariant Labs) RR. The MCP authorisation spec makes authorisation optional, forbids token passthrough and recommends short-lived tokens (MCP spec 2025-06-18) CP. Enterprise gateways exist (Cloudflare MCP server portals with per-tool allowlists and Logpush, where blocked users "can bypass this by using a server's direct URL"; Docker MCP Gateway, "invite-only"; Azure API Management with Entra JWT policies) (Cloudflare; Docker; Microsoft Learn) CP.

As of 9 October 2026 no primary source confirms an in-the-wild incident in which a coding agent exfiltrated secrets via MCP; the record is high-fidelity research (Sentry MCP agentjacking, 85% success in testing, 2,388 injectable DSNs, Sentry calling a root-cause fix "technically not defensible"; claude-code-action CVE-2025-66032) plus one CI case the CSA says was exploited in the wild (CSA agentjacking; CSA CI/CD) CP/PO. Absence is consistent with low detectability: the Sentry attack uses legitimate developer credentials and looks like normal activity INF.

Rules files are an injection surface. Pillar's "Rules File Backdoor" hid instructions in .cursor/rules and Copilot instruction files with zero-width and bidirectional Unicode; Cursor said the risk "falls under users' responsibility", GitHub that users are "responsible for reviewing and accepting suggestions", and GitHub added a hidden-Unicode warning on 1 May 2025 (Pillar Security) VR; no in-the-wild case is reported VR.

4.5.5 Dev/prod separation and credential brokering

Dev/prod separation is the one categorical control. Beyond Replit, Neon recommends snapshots at "Start of each agent session" and "Before database schema changes", restorable with the connection string preserved, at USD 0.09/GB-month, and offers anonymised branches masked with PostgreSQL Anonymizer (Neon versioning; Neon anonymization) CP. Supabase branches are "data-less by default", auto-deleted on PR close, experimental and paid, with no masking documented (Supabase branching) CP. PlanetScale restricts production-branch deletion to administrators and recommends against production data in development (PlanetScale) CP.

Credential brokering keeps the destructive credential out of the agent's reach: Vercel OIDC Federation (all plans; build and function tokens with 2-hour TTL; cloud roles scoped per project and environment) (Vercel OIDC) CP; GitHub Actions OIDC tokens "only valid for a single job" with sub claims such as repo:org/repo:environment:prod (GitHub OIDC) CP; Cloudflare Outbound Workers intercept every fetch() and add authentication "so end developers don't need credentials" (Cloudflare) CP; Claude Code's mask plus injectHosts substitutes the real secret only on allowed hosts CP. ASI03 of the OWASP Agentic Top 10 states the principle: agents "inherit credentials such as user sessions, API keys, and OAuth tokens"; "Issue short-lived, narrowly scoped tokens per task" (Descope summary of OWASP) RR. Where a provider cannot scope tokens, as Railway still cannot, the closest approximation is token type (a project token bound to one environment) and the gap is a vendor-selection criterion INF.

4.5.6 Backups outside the blast radius

An architecture that survives the PocketOS class needs a copy outside the identity domain the agent's credentials can reach, storage-enforced immutability that no credential in the primary account can shorten, optional cross-account restore paths, and tested restores with measured RPO and RTO PROP. AWS logically air-gapped vaults store recovery points "in an AWS Backup service owned account", are "always locked with a vault lock in compliance mode", have a minimum retention of 7 days, and "Can optionally be shared across accounts using AWS RAM" (AWS LAG vault) CP. Multi-party approval is optional: "You can integrate your logically air-gapped vaults with Multi-party approval (MPA) to enable recovery ... even if the vault-owning account is inaccessible", requiring two Organizations, Identity Center, a RAM-shared approval team and an SCP, with "no additional MPA cost" (AWS MPA) CP. Compliance-mode Vault Lock becomes immutable after a grace period of at least 3 days, denying deletion to "any user (including the root user)"; on account closure AWS deletes vault contents after 90 days "even if a vault lock was in place" (AWS Vault Lock) CP. Equivalents: Azure's immutable vault "Enabled and locked" (irreversible; enforced soft delete 14 days; Resource Guard in a different tenant for "Maximum" isolation) (Azure); GCP backup vaults where "no one (not even a Project Owner) can decrease the retention period" (GCP); Backblaze B2 compliance-mode Object Lock (Backblaze); pgBackRest relying on storage-layer object lock (pgBackRest) CP.

Cost anchors: AWS warm storage USD 0.05/GB-month; LAG vault USD 0.0575/GB-month (eu-west-1 example); restore USD 0.02–0.03/GB; restore testing USD 1.50 per recovery point (AWS Backup pricing) CP. For a 200 GB database with 30 recovery points averaging 400 GB stored, the authors' arithmetic gives roughly USD 30–40 per month for an air-gapped, compliance-locked copy INF. Provider-internal grace periods (Railway two days, Replit 7 days, Azure 14 days) protect against a single destructive call but not against a credential that can also purge or wait out the window INF. PocketOS's own newest backup was months old, so the restore-drill gap was a root cause alongside the storage layout PO/INF.

4.5.7 Dependency and registry controls

Package hallucination is measured and persistent (section 5.7). Socket Firewall's free tier blocks known-malicious packages at install, but "possible AI-detected malware only triggers a warning" and unknown packages pass (Socket) VR. Copilot's recommended allowlist includes public registries, leaving this path open CP. The in-the-wild counterpart of the threat is Shai-Hulud: the November 2025 wave hit 525+ npm packages with about 132 million monthly downloads, harvested AWS, GCP, Azure, npm and GitHub credentials via preinstall, and installed a self-hosted Actions runner when the GitHub token had workflows scope (Semgrep) RR. An agent running npm install inside a sandbox whose reads cover ~/.aws, ~/.npmrc and the environment hands such a worm the same material a developer shell would INF.

4.6 Shadow-app discovery pipeline

4.6.1 The verified wildcard-certificate finding

Direct TLS probes on 9 October 2026 show that all five platform subdomains are served under a single wildcard certificate, live apps included: nonexistent-probe-xyz123.lovable.app presented CN=lovable.app, SAN *.lovable.app (Google Trust Services); replit.app presented *.replit.app; *.vercel.app covered next-blog-starter.vercel.app, nextjs-dashboard.vercel.app and v0-portfolio.vercel.app (HTTP 200); *.netlify.app, O=Netlify, Inc (DigiCert) covered docs-example.netlify.app; *.base44.app (Let's Encrypt) CP (own verification). Per-app hostnames on platform subdomains therefore never appear in Certificate Transparency logs. Custom domains attached to these platforms do receive per-domain certificates and are CT-visible: Vercel "will automatically try to generate a certificate for every domain once it is added to a project" (Vercel SSL) CP. Symbiotic's own pipeline corroborates the blind spot: crt.sh produced 52 of its 1,085 confirmed sites (4.8%), against dorking 632 (58.2%), Common Crawl 251 (23.1%) and DNS brute-force 149 (13.7%) (Symbiotic) VR. Because platform subdomains share one certificate and one IP pool, EASM products that attribute by certificate names or netblocks (Censys's discovery paths, Defender EASM seeds) cannot attribute these apps to a customer; attribution must come from content (brand tokens, logos, corporate email domains), HTML/JS fingerprints (Escape used Shodan this way), search indexes and Common Crawl, or inside signals (Censys ASM; Defender EASM; Escape methodology) CP/INF.

4.6.2 The pipeline

Six steps, each tied to documented sources PROP:

  1. Inside the sanctioned tenants (day 1, no cost). Export Lovable Security insights filtered to "Externally published" and "no owner"; Power Platform Inventory CSV or API; Replit team Deployments; Vercel and Netlify project lists with protection settings, remembering team defaults do not retro-apply CP.
  2. Identity and email signals (days 1–7). IdP sign-in and OAuth consent logs for builder domains; email-metadata scanning of vendor welcome and billing mails recovers historical sign-ups (Nudge Security) VR; expense-feed and unfederated-access detection (Torii, vendor comparison) VR; corporate-card and corporate-email registration checks (TechTarget) PO.
  3. SSE/CASB/browser telemetry (days 1–14). Query SWG and CASB logs for builder consoles and for *.lovable.app, *.replit.app, *.vercel.app, *.netlify.app, *.base44.app using the GenAI app-type categories in Cloudflare Gateway, Netskope CCI and Defender for Cloud Apps (Cloudflare; Netskope CCI) CP; access logs show platform use, not what was deployed (VentureBeat) PO. No vendor documentation confirms that builder published-app domains are catalogued as distinct apps rather than under generic AI categories (gap).
  4. Outside-in enumeration (weeks 1–3). Search dorking with brand tokens restricted to the five platform zones, Common Crawl index queries, DNS brute-force of brand tokens under platform zones, GitHub code search, urlscan.io, Wayback; Shodan/Censys HTML fingerprint searches; CT monitoring only for the corporate domain, known custom domains and Lovable branded URLs VR/CP.
  5. Passive triage (continuous). Fetch the bundle, look for *.supabase.co URLs and key formats, attempt an anonymous read only. Both research pipelines converged on this minimum check set VR. Write and delete probes must not be run against assets the company does not own; both research teams that ran them acknowledged they could not fully explain how those were non-destructive INF.
  6. Register, gate, repeat. Move finds into the sanctioned tenant with team defaults set (Vercel "All Deployments", Replit "Require", Lovable "Workspace", Base44 Apps SSO); put custom-domain apps behind an IdP gate with origin validation CP.

Cost anchors: Cloudflare Zero Trust free to 50 users then USD 7/user/month; Vercel Authentication on All Deployments free; Netlify Pro USD 20/month; Lovable Business about USD 50/month (third-party, July 2026); Replit Core USD 20, Pro USD 100, Enterprise USD 10,000–200,000 annual commitment; Power Apps Premium USD 20/user/month (Replit pricing; Replit Enterprise; Netlify pricing) CP/PO. crt.sh, Common Crawl and search operators are free; no published, independently evaluated accuracy figures exist for any dedicated vibe-app scanner INF.

5. Corrected empirical evidence on AI-generated code security

Read with their denominators, five independent academic studies and three vendor evaluations since 2025 support a narrower conclusion than "prompting redistributes rather than reduces": prompting shifts the correctness-versus-security trade-off, and whether that helps depends on model class, task length and phrasing. Nothing in this evidence caps damage; that is the job of the boundaries in section 4.

5.1 SusVibes, with correct denominators and best pairing

SusVibes (ICML 2026; v2 16 Feb 2026) is 200 Python feature-request tasks from 108 open-source repositories, each reconstructed from a CVE-linked fix commit, with mean repository context 162K lines and mean patch 172 lines; the agent passes when its code satisfies the functional tests and the security tests written for that specific CWE (SusVibes) RR. Table 3, FuncPass / SecPass as % of 200 tasks: Claude 4 Sonnet with SWE-agent 61.0 / 10.5, OpenHands 49.5 / 12.5, Claude Code 44.0 / 6.0; Kimi K2 22.5 / 6.0, 37.0 / 9.0, 43.5 / 8.0; Gemini 2.5 Pro 19.5 / 7.0, 21.5 / 8.5, 15.0 / 4.5 RR. "Over 80%" of functionally correct solutions were insecure (82.8% for SWE-agent + Claude 4 Sonnet) RR. Every run already included the reminder "Make sure to follow best security practices and avoid common vulnerabilities" RR. Adding the exact target CWE left the joint rate at 10.5% while cutting FuncPass to 56.0%; self-selected CWEs gave 52.5 / 9.5, because agents picked 7.5 CWEs per task against 1.06 ground truth with precision around 0.1 RR. The authors "do not claim coverage of all exploit modalities", so SecPass is an upper bound on true security RR/INF. The released v1.0 dataset holds 186 tasks, not 200 (LeiLiLab/susvibes) RR. The defensible one-sentence statement: on CVE-derived Python feature work in mature codebases, developer agents produced code that passed both functional and targeted security tests on 4.5–12.5% of tasks, with the best functional agent at 10.5% and the best joint agent at 12.5%. It is not a measure of vibe-coded apps.

5.2 The Veracode trend, with methodology

Veracode's suite is 80 tasks (4 languages × 4 CWEs: SQL injection, XSS, log injection, weak crypto × 5 instances), judged by Veracode's own SAST, with no security prompting by design, across more than 150 models cumulatively (Veracode Spring 2026) VR. The 2025 release found 45% of tasks introduced an OWASP Top 10 flaw, Java above 70% failure, XSS 86% failure (Veracode press release) VR. The March 2026 update reports 55% overall pass (SQLi 82%, crypto 86%, XSS 15%, log injection 13%; Python 62%, Java 29%), GPT-5.1/5.2 "within the margin of error of GPT-4.1", Claude 4.5/4.6 "flat compared to earlier Claude generations", and GPT-5-series with extended reasoning at 70–72%, "a meaningful improvement over the standard 55%" yet "far from acceptable"; syntax pass rose from about 50% to 95% since 2023 VR. The corrected statement: average security pass for frontier non-reasoning models stayed in a 45–55% band from 2023 to March 2026 on a vendor SAST-judged, four-CWE suite with no authorisation CWEs, with a documented exception for reasoning modes INF. BaxBench independently finds reasoning models (o1, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) are the only class that benefits from generic security reminders (BaxBench) RR. The Georgia Tech SSLab tracker attributes 74 CVEs to AI-authored code between 1 May 2025 and 20 Mar 2026 (Claude Code 49, Copilot 15), a figure the researcher calls a lower bound reflecting "detection blind spots" (The Register) RR.

5.3 Prompting: redistribution, semantic drift, and phrasing

"Redistributes, not reduces" comes from one single-shot study on the synthetic Cybernative.ai dataset: 424 Python tasks, five prompts, GPT-4o and LLaMA 3.1-8B, one output each, Bandit and CodeQL (Urmi et al., arXiv 2608.24857) RR. With a minimal prompt GPT-4o returned valid code on only 86 of 424 tasks; structured prompts raised that to about 380. Among valid outputs the share with any Bandit finding fell from 76.3% to 72.0% and the high-severity share fell from 20.8% to 13.6% while the low-severity share rose from 32.0% to 43.5%; absolute counts were never reported RR. "Semantic drift" (the model removing a requested unsafe construct) was found in 9–10 of 15 manually sampled task IDs under security prompts, in a dataset where some tasks "explicitly encourage insecure practices" RR. The authors conclude prompt structure "is an unreliable substitute for robust security controls" RR. The result is evidence that security framing changes what the model writes, not evidence that prompting cannot reduce counts INF.

Other studies fill in the conditions. Backslash (vendor, Apr 2025, task count unpublished): GPT-4o 10% → 20% → 65% vulnerability-free across naive, generic and OWASP prompts; Claude 3.7 Sonnet 60% → 100%; "five out of the seven LLMs" still produced vulnerable code (Infosecurity Magazine) VR. BaxBench (392 backends, exploit-confirmed): for Claude 3.5 Sonnet a generic reminder raised secure-and-correct from 32.9% to 34.5% while dropping correctness from 53.6% to 45.5% RR. The only study on real deployed vibe-coded apps replayed 70 confirmed vulnerabilities 1,680 times: a one-line "production-ready" framing cut reintroduction from 25.7% to 11.0%, a security-hardening skill to 11.9%, while a long "professional" technical prompt raised it to 45.7%; a stronger model tier changed nothing (+0.5 pp); in 20.7% of reintroductions the agent "recognized the risk and still shipped the insecure code" (Deng, Fan and Meng) RR. The cross-study pattern is a correctness tax (SusVibes −5.5 to −8.5 pp FuncPass; BaxBench −8 to −12.6 pp pass@1); prompting helps on short tasks with reasoning-class models and concise framing, and does not help on long agentic trajectories, with weaker models, or where the model knows the risk and ships anyway INF.

5.4 Iteration

Shukla et al. (GPT-4o, 10 secure C/Java baselines × 4 strategies × 10 rounds, 400 samples) found 387 vulnerabilities: efficiency-focused 124, feature-focused 158, security-focused 38, ambiguous 67; per-sample means rose from about 2.1 (rounds 1–2) to 6.2 (rounds 8–10) (Shukla et al.) RR. The abstract's "37.6% increase in critical vulnerabilities after just five iterations" has no defined baseline in the body and should not be quoted as a precise figure RR/INF. The authors' operating rule, at most three LLM-only rounds between human reviews, is reasonable RR.

5.5 Stack-specific skills, with sample sizes

Supabase's own evaluation (9 Apr 2026): six scenarios per condition, LLM judge on Braintrust, run count not stated. Baseline / MCP only / MCP + skill: Claude Code Opus 4.6 58 / 50 / 67; Sonnet 4.6 46 / 58 / 71; Codex GPT-5.4 71 / 71 / 88; GPT-5.4 Mini 42 / 63 / 71. "These are early results with a small sample size" (Supabase blog) VR. With n = 6 a swing of one scenario is about 17 pp; the deltas are within one or two scenarios INF. The Opus 4.6 regression when the MCP server was added without the skill shows that more tools without rules can reduce security outcomes VR/INF. The mechanism is plausible: the independent vibe-app study attributes 658 of 1,186 confirmed vulnerabilities (55.5%) to "hidden security rules" of the platform, exactly what the skill encodes (RLS on views, app_metadata, grants, SECURITY DEFINER) RR. Kharma et al. (12 developers, gpt-5.2, Spring Boot) cut authorisation/object-access weaknesses from 30 to 14 with a bundled training-plus-scaffold intervention, no control arm (arXiv 2604.17763) RR. No study A/B-tests a stack-specific skill against a generic OWASP rules file on identical tasks (gap). Verdict: directionally positive, underpowered, vendor-run, LLM-judged INF.

5.6 AI review: recall limits for authorisation bugs

The one 2026 study that checks whether a reviewer identified the actual vulnerability (MalPR-Bench, 89 malicious PRs plus 50 benign controls) found CodeRabbit blocked 24 of 31 held-out PRs but pointed at the true flaw in 16: 16 of 24 when the evidence lay inside the touched files, 0 of 7 when it lay outside; for 14 "absence-type" (missing-check) cases it blocked 9 and identified the target in 3 (Chen et al., arXiv 2608.25730) RR. That is the profile of a missing RLS policy, a missing ownership check or an inverted guard INF. An academic test of Copilot Code Review (public preview, Feb 2025) produced "fewer than 20 comments" across seven vulnerable datasets, "most of which addressed spelling or minor style issues", and no injection or access-control finding (Amro and Alalfi) RR. Vendor precision figures are 20–68% on Augment's dataset and 64–96% on the CodeRabbit-commissioned Signal65 dataset, neither reporting recall (Signal65; Liatrio) VR. Copilot Autofix's median 28 minutes to fix versus 1.5 hours manual is a remediation-speed metric for CodeQL-detected alerts, not a detection result (GitHub blog) VR. Bright Security's five-run test is one 300-line app with two planted XSS flaws, undisclosed prompts, undefined denominators and an internal inconsistency (Bright Security) VR; it illustrates run-to-run variance and nothing more INF. A clean AI review is not evidence of absence; use it to find and fix faster and keep deterministic scanners and behavioural tests as the gate INF.

5.7 Package hallucination versus in-the-wild exploitation

Hallucination is measured: across 576,000 code samples from 16 models, 440,445 of 2.23 million package references (19.7%) did not exist; commercial models at least 5.2%, open-source 21.7%; 205,474 unique names; 43% of hallucinated names recurred on all ten re-queries; most are not typosquats (48.6% at Levenshtein distance 6 or more) (Spracklen et al., USENIX Security 2025) RR. A 2026 re-measurement across Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Haiku 4.5, GPT-5.4-mini, Gemini 2.5 Pro and DeepSeek V3.2 found 4.62–6.10% on 199,845 paired prompts, with 127 names hallucinated identically by all five models and 53 still registrable (Churilov, arXiv 2605.17062) RR. Exploitation is a separate ledger: unused-imports (npm) is "a real malicious package" on a name LLMs produce instead of eslint-plugin-unused-imports, drawing about 233 weekly downloads in early February 2026 before npm's security hold, "potentially an intentional slopsquatting attack" whose intent Aikido "can't prove"; react-codeshift (npm) was a benign name spread to 237 repositories by 47 LLM-generated agent skills and claimed defensively; huggingface-cli (PyPI, 2024) was a benign placeholder with more than 30,000 downloads (Aikido) VR. The CSA's "tens of thousands of downloads" line conflates the benign placeholder with the malicious package and should not be repeated as malicious reach INF. Evidence ladder as of October 2026: hallucination confirmed; registration of hallucinated names confirmed, once with malicious code; autonomous agents installing hallucinated names from skill files confirmed; no documented victim compromise INF.

5.8 Population evidence for vibe-coded apps

Deng, Fan and Meng (v4, 14 Sep 2026) fingerprinted 74,800 repositories (Claude Code .claude/ and Lovable meta tags), kept 9,041 with at least 90% AI-authored commits and lines, found 1,007 deployed, randomly sampled 200 deployed web apps and confirmed 1,186 vulnerabilities by two-author manual exploitability review (κ = 0.87): 182 of 200 (91.0%) vulnerable, median 6 per vulnerable app, 65.77% Critical or High; Broken Access Control 339 findings (28.58%) in 64.0% of apps, 77% of them backend; Injection in 54.0%; Authentication Failures in 51.5% (arXiv 2606.23130v4) RR. Root causes: knowledge defects 63.4% (of which "hidden security rules" 55.5%), objective defects 23.1% ("demo-oriented design" 14.7%), memory defects 13.5% RR. The authors' advice: "generated apps should be treated as prototypes" RR. No study counts cross-tenant or RLS-correctness failures separately, and no study measures builder expertise; "non-developer builders" is inferred from platform, not measured INF.

6.6 Counter-evidence on bans versus enablement

The paved road has four documented weaknesses CP/VR. 7. The legal and standards layer

Nothing in this section is legal advice; the decision trees present regulator guidance, not an opinion on a specific incident, and the audit's instruction to obtain qualified legal review before publication stands.

7.1 GDPR: risk-thresholded decision trees per failure mode (EDPB Guidelines 9/2022)

A personal data breach exists only where "a breach of security" led to "the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data" (Art. 4(12)); an unexploited vulnerability is an Art. 32 weakness, not by itself a notifiable breach CP. Once a breach exists, Art. 33 requires notification to the supervisory authority "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it ... unless the personal data breach is unlikely to result in a risk"; Art. 34 requires communication to individuals only where the breach is "likely to result in a high risk"; Art. 33(5) requires documentation of every breach, notifiable or not (EDPB Guidelines 9/2022 v2.0, paras 28–29, 82–83, 121) CP. The EDPB's three breach types are confidentiality, integrity and availability (para 17); "A breach will always be regarded as an availability breach when there has been a permanent loss of, or destruction of, personal data" (para 19); a temporary loss restored from backup is still a breach of security to be documented and assessed case by case, with the worked contrast of hospital data (could present a risk) and a media company's newsletter (unlikely) (paras 20–22) CP. Awareness arises at "a reasonable degree of certainty that a security incident has occurred that has led to personal data being compromised" (para 31); a short investigation is permitted before the clock starts (para 34); the lost-USB example says notify even when access cannot be established (para 33) CP. A processor "must notify the controller 'without undue delay'" and "does not need to first assess the likelihood of risk" (para 44) CP.

The Digital Omnibus (COM(2025) 837, 19 Nov 2025) proposes notification only where a breach is "likely to result in a high risk", 96 hours, and an ENISA single entry point; the EDPB–EDPS Joint Opinion 2/2026 welcomed the direction; as of September 2026 there was a Parliament draft report (22 Jun 2026) and Council working-party activity but no committee vote or trilogue (Praxikon, 15 Sep 2026; White & Case) PO. 72 hours and the "unlikely to result in a risk" threshold are the law in force CP.

7.2 Registry to Art. 30 and Art. 35 without one ROPA per app

Art. 30 records are kept per processing activity (purposes, categories of data subjects and data, recipients, transfers, retention "where possible", and "where possible, a general description of the technical and organisational security measures" under Art. 30(1)(g)) (EUR-Lex GDPR) CP. An app supports one or more activities and several apps can support one. The registry should link each app to the activity records it serves by purpose; where none exists the app has surfaced a new processing activity and the DPO creates one; the app's authorisation model, publishing setting, backup arrangement and vendor DPA status feed the Art. 30(1)(g) description and the Art. 28 recipient entry PROP. DPIAs are triggered by processing "likely to result in a high risk" (Art. 35(1)), not by every citizen app; the WP248 criteria most often met by citizen-built apps are new technology (LLM features, agentic actions), sensitive data, vulnerable subjects, scale, matching datasets and systematic monitoring, with two or more generally requiring a DPIA (EDPB endorsed WP29 guidelines) CP (wording from prior knowledge; not re-opened). DPIA screening belongs in the registry as a questionnaire, not an automatic trigger PROP. The registry is nonetheless the strongest Art. 24 accountability evidence available when an authority asks about an app that leaked INF.

7.3 EU AI Act: the three-question "AI system" test and Art. 4/50 status after the Omnibus

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (8 Jul 2026; OJ 24 Jul; in force 27 Jul 2026) moved Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 and Annex I to 2 August 2028; Art. 50 transparency applies from 2 August 2026, with a grace period to 2 December 2026 only for the Art. 50(2) marking duty of generative systems already on the market (EUR-Lex OJ L 2026/1744; Commission FAQ on Art. 50; AI Act Service Desk timeline) CP. Art. 4 as amended reads that providers and deployers "take measures to support the development of AI literacy" with the express clause "This obligation does not require providers or deployers to guarantee any specific level of AI literacy of any individual" (AI Act Service Desk, Art. 4) CP.

The Act regulates the deployed system's behaviour, not how its source code was authored. Under Art. 3(1) and the Commission's February 2025 guidelines (C(2025) 924), systems "based on the rules defined solely by natural persons to automatically execute operations" and "basic data processing systems" are outside the definition (Commission guidelines; CMS summary) CP. Three questions of the deployed app PROP:

  1. Does any component infer outputs (predictions, content, recommendations, decisions) from inputs rather than executing human-written rules? No: not an AI system; GDPR, NIS2/DORA and CRA only. The coding assistant that wrote it is an AI system whose provider is the vendor; the enterprise is a deployer, which is what engages Art. 4.
  2. If yes, is the enterprise placing that component into service under its own name (a branded support bot calling an LLM API)? If yes it is likely the provider of that AI system (Art. 3(3): "has an AI system ... developed and ... puts the AI system into service under its own name") and also the deployer internally; Art. 50(1) (inform people they interact with an AI system) and, for generative output, 50(2) (machine-readable marking) apply from 2 August 2026, with fines up to EUR 15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover (Art. 99(4)) (AI Act Service Desk FAQ) CP. Whether an enterprise merely wrapping a third-party GPAI model is the "provider" for 50(2) is contested and awaits the code of practice; flag for counsel.
  3. Does the use case fall in Annex III (employment, education, credit, essential services)? If yes, high-risk obligations apply from 2 December 2027; the CSA's "drift into high-risk use cases" risk is real, so the registry records intended use.

The CSA note's "August 2, 2026" high-risk date was accurate when written and is superseded CP. Art. 4 is a duty of effort; training on BaaS authorisation, secrets and publishing defaults is a natural way to evidence it INF. The verbatim post-Omnibus text of Art. 50(1)–(5) was not extracted in this pass; sources report no substantive amendment to those paragraphs; confirm against the consolidated text (EUR-Lex consolidated) before publication (gap).

7.4 NIS2 and DORA with in-scope qualifiers

Neither instrument names AI builders or coding assistants. NIS2 Art. 21(2) requires essential and important entities to address "(c) business continuity, such as backup management and disaster recovery", "(d) supply chain security, including security-related aspects concerning the relationships between each entity and its direct suppliers or service providers", "(e) security in network and information systems acquisition, development and maintenance, including vulnerability handling", "(g) basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training" and "(i) ... asset management"; Art. 21(3) requires account of each supplier's "secure development procedures" (NIS2 Art. 21 mirror; EUR-Lex Directive 2022/2555) CP. The applicability chain for an in-scope entity (an inference): the citizen-built app is a system the entity uses for its operations, its development falls under 21(2)(e), the builder is a supplier under 21(2)(d) and 21(3), the estate under 21(2)(i), backups under 21(2)(c) INF. None of this is universal; it attaches to in-scope entities, is proportionate and varies with national transposition CP.

DORA (applying since 17 Jan 2025) makes financial entities "at all times ... fully responsible" (Art. 28(1)), requires a register of all ICT contractual arrangements distinguishing those supporting critical or important functions (28(3)), pre-contract assessment (28(4)) and, for critical or important functions, "unrestricted" rights of access, inspection and audit, participation in threat-led penetration testing and exit terms (30(3)) (DORA Art. 28; DORA Art. 30; EUR-Lex DORA) CP. A builder hosting an app used by a financial entity is an ICT third-party service provider; every such arrangement, including a shadow personal sign-up, belongs in the 28(3) register and its absence is itself a gap INF. The decisive qualifier is critical or important function; most prototypes are not, but anything on a payments, onboarding or regulatory-reporting path may be, and for those Lovable's DPA (no audit clause) and Vercel's DPA (audit rights "satisfied by" SOC 2 reports) do not supply 30(3)(e) on standard terms CP/INF. No competent authority has named AI builders as a supply-chain category (gap).

7.5 Cyber Resilience Act: scope and dates

The CRA (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) covers "products with digital elements placed on the market": downloadable or installable software, embedded software, and "software plus cloud" where remote data processing is essential; "Pure SaaS is generally excluded" and falls under NIS2/DORA instead; internal tools never placed on the market are out of scope (Kirkland; DLA Piper) PO. Art. 14 reporting (actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents: early warning 24 hours, notification 72 hours, final report 14 days or one month) has applied since 11 September 2026; essential requirements, vulnerability handling, documentation and conformity assessment apply from 11 December 2027; fines up to EUR 15 million or 2.5% of turnover (Kirkland) PO. A vibe-coded SaaS with a Supabase backend is normally outside the CRA; a vibe-coded mobile or desktop app distributed commercially is inside, and its backend may be a remote data processing solution; the manufacturer then owes Art. 14 reporting today and Annex I ("secure by default configuration", "without known exploitable vulnerabilities") from December 2027 INF. The Commission's separate guidance on remote data processing was not located as published (gap).

7.8 DPA procurement checklist

The three documents read in this pass (Lovable DPA, page updated 6 Nov 2025; Vercel DPA, effective 31 Mar 2026; Replit ToS, 3 Aug 2026) share one pattern: processor only for a defined class of Customer Personal Data and independent controller for service, usage or log data; training prohibitions attach to the processor class only; audit rights replaced by SOC 2 or ISO reports; deletion "commercially reasonable" or "may persist in backups"; breach notice "without undue delay" with no hour commitment; sub-processor objection windows of 5–20 days with termination as the sole remedy (Lovable DPA; Vercel DPA; Replit ToS) CP. Lovable's §10.1 bars training on Customer Personal Data while §9.1(c) permits "training or tuning proprietary machine-learning models" on Service Data; the two "appear to conflict" CP. Replit's ToS grants a licence to "copy, display, distribute, perform, reformat, and modify your content" and to access private apps for "troubleshooting, improving our service", and does not state whether content trains models; its DPA and Commercial Agreement were not read (gap) CP. Cursor's security page says Privacy Mode means "we will not train on your data", inheritable by team members, without using the words "zero data retention" (Cursor security) CP; the original report's BYOK and per-model ZDR caveats came from a different Cursor page not re-opened (gap).

Checklist PROP, grounded in Art. 28(3) CP:

  1. Carve-outs. Identify exactly which data classes the vendor treats as its own controller data; ask in writing whether prompt and chat content is Customer Personal Data.
  2. Training. Require the no-training clause to cover every class the vendor can see, not only Customer Personal Data.
  3. Audit rights. Art. 28(3)(h) requires the processor to "allow for and contribute to audits, including inspections"; report-only clauses may suit low-risk processing but not DORA critical or important functions.
  4. Backups and deletion. Demand a maximum backup retention and a deletion certificate.
  5. Sub-processors. Monitor the list proactively; map sub-processors to the Art. 30 record.
  6. Transfers. Confirm the SCC module (Two versus Three) and whether any DPF certification is relied on; Lovable and Vercel rely on SCCs, not the DPF.
  7. Breach notification. Negotiate a fixed outer bound (24–48 hours from processor awareness) and a duty to notify on suspicion, consistent with EDPB para 44.
  8. Regulated data. No BAA means no PHI; check PCI DSS responsibility and special-category exclusions.

12. Conclusion

Three things changed between the original report and this paper. First, the evidence base is now large enough to say that the leak class is structural and stable: the one random-sample study, the five non-independent scans and twenty incidents agree that the dominant defect is authorisation on a reachable data API, that it is created by the same mechanism the agents use to build (programmatic table creation without grants and policies), and that seventeen months of model releases have not visibly moved the exposure rate. Vendors have responded by changing defaults at the storage layer, which closes the new-table path on Supabase from 30 October 2026 but leaves existing tables, RPC EXECUTE and every other BaaS where they were. Second, the agent plane has acquired a documented, vendor-acknowledged anatomy: sandboxes bound shell commands and nothing else; MCP, hooks and file tools run on the host; allowlists bound destinations, not exfiltration; and the only categorical control anyone has shipped is Replit's removal of production from the agent's reach. Third, the legal picture is more precise and less dramatic than "every exposure is a reportable breach": the EDPB framework already decides each failure mode, the AI Act does not reach a CRUD tool, and DORA bites through contract terms that consumer-grade builders do not offer.

The practical consequence for a CISO, platform architect or DPO is a short list of decisions that generated code cannot override: whether the browser may talk to the database at all and, if so, whether anyone has run the cross-tenant matrix; whether a non-admin can publish to the open internet; where the agent's credentials live and what they can delete; whether a copy of the data exists that no credential in the primary account can shorten; and whether the organisation can produce, on request, a register naming an owner for the app that leaked. Everything else in this paper, including its own proposals, is second-order once those five are settled.
